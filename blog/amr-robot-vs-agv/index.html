AMR vs AGV: What’s the Difference and Which Should You Choose?

An AGV follows a guide path someone has engineered into the floor or the site; an AMR builds a map and plans its own route around people and obstacles. Choose an AGV when the route is fixed, protected and will not change for years. Choose an AMR when pickup points move, aisles are shared with people and forklifts, or one fleet has to handle several kinds of job. Price, safety and integration all follow from that one decision.

Quick answer: choose around route stability

If the material route is predictable, protected from people and unlikely to change, an AGV is a sound fixed-route transport system. If the site changes slotting, shares aisles with people and forklifts, adds pickup points or wants one fleet to do several jobs, an AMR is usually the more adaptable and cheaper-to-change choice.

Many buyers start by asking whether an AMR is simply “newer” than an AGV. That is the wrong question. A well-engineered AGV loop can run for a decade in a stable plant, and an AMR can disappoint in a site that cannot keep its aisles clear. The useful question is whether the vehicle should follow a path the site builds for it, or decide its own path from on-board perception and a map.

What is the difference between an AMR and an AGV?

An automated guided vehicle (AGV) follows a defined guide path: magnetic tape, an embedded wire, floor QR codes, reflectors or transponders. Once the lane, pickup points, speed rules and stop zones are engineered, the vehicle repeats a narrow transport job with very predictable behaviour. If something blocks the path, it stops and waits.

An autonomous mobile robot (AMR) localises itself with on-board sensors — lidar, cameras, inertial sensors and wheel odometry — against a map, then plans its own route. When an aisle is blocked it can slow, wait or take another route, and new stations are added by editing the map rather than the floor. Our AMR-0300, for example, fuses visual SLAM with lidar SLAM and reaches ±5 cm standard positioning, or ±1 cm at precise connection points.

The line between the two is blurring. Some modern AGVs have good obstacle detection and fleet control; some AMRs are run in disciplined, AGV-like lanes; and many AMRs can be switched between free, tracked and hybrid navigation. Compare the operating envelope, not the label: how the vehicle localises, what it does when blocked, how maps are changed, and who validates the final route.

AMR vs AGV price: what actually drives the cost

Neither technology is automatically cheaper. AGV projects put more money into the floor and route engineering; AMR projects put more into software, mapping and integration. The number that matters is the cost per automated route over the life of the workflow — including what it costs to change that route.

Quoted unit prices are hard to compare, because suppliers bundle very different things: one quote includes chargers, fleet software and commissioning, another lists the bare vehicle. Before comparing, put every quote on the same basis.

Then size the benefit: trips per shift taken off people, empty walking removed, forklift movements avoided in busy aisles, waiting time at conveyors and lifts, and night-shift continuity. If the layout will change even once or twice over the system’s life, include that change in the comparison — it is often what decides AMR versus AGV.

Which technology fits each workflow?

AMRs do well when tasks vary: replenishment in the morning, empty-container returns later, finished-goods staging at night and ad-hoc deliveries at peaks. AGVs do well when the job looks like a conveyor on wheels: the same start, the same destination, the same lane discipline and a steady takt time.

Mezzanines, lifts and multi-floor sites

On mezzanines and multi-floor sites, the robot is rarely the limit. The limits are the floor’s load rating, unprotected edges, thresholds and gaps at lift doors, and whether the lift can be controlled by the fleet system.

AGVs and AMRs both run on mezzanines, but the risk assessment must cover the drop. Check that every edge the vehicle can approach has guarding or a mapped keep-out zone backed by safety-rated sensing, and that the mezzanine is rated for the robot plus its heaviest load and any dynamic braking forces.

Moving between floors means using a lift. AMRs are usually better placed here, because lift calling, door control and floor switching are part of the fleet software — the AMR-0300 and AMR-0600 both list lift and e-door integration, and the AMR-0600 schedules lifts by giving idle lifts priority. Check the physical details too: the threshold step and gap at each lift door against the robot’s limits (20 mm step and 35 mm gap on the AMR-0300; 10 mm step on the AMR-0600), and the lift door width against the robot and load (at least 700 mm for the AMR-0300R, 800 mm for the AMR-0600R).

→Mezzanine floor rating covers robot, load and braking forces.
→Every open edge is guarded or protected by a validated keep-out zone.
→Lift thresholds, gaps and door widths are measured against the robot’s limits.
→Lift and door control interfaces are agreed with the building owner and lift maintainer.
→Fire alarm behaviour is defined: where robots park and how lifts are released.

Payload, docking and software questions not to skip

Payload is more than a maximum kilogram figure. A 300 kg rating does not tell you whether the robot can carry a tall rack, an off-centre load or a trolley with poor castors. Ask for the payload envelope: centre-of-gravity limits, acceleration and braking assumptions, ramp and threshold limits, floor flatness, turning space and how the load is retained.

Docking is the other common failure point. Conveyor transfer, rack lifting and cart pickup need different tolerances; the AMR-0300 conveyor version, for instance, is specified to ±10 mm and ±1° docking accuracy. For each station, define the final pose tolerance, how the robot confirms it has docked, what happens on timeout, how staff recover a failed transfer, and who owns the interface if the conveyor changes.

Integrate software in stages. A pilot can start with manual dispatch or call buttons, but a production fleet needs tasks from the WMS, MES or ERP, plus rules for priority, cancellation, exceptions, charging and map versions. If you run robots from more than one vendor, ask about VDA 5050, the open interface between vehicles and a master controller; the AMR-0600 supports it. Safety standards and the AMR / AGV risk assessment

AMRs and AGVs are both driverless industrial trucks in the eyes of the main safety standard. ISO 3691-4:2023 sets safety requirements and verification for driverless industrial trucks and their systems, explicitly including both AGVs and AMRs. In North America, ANSI/A3 R15.08 covers industrial mobile robots in two parts — requirements for the robot manufacturer, and requirements for integration and the user’s site. A3’s mobile robot safety material makes the key point: safety is an application-level responsibility, not just a property of the robot. OSHA’s robotics technical manual records why mixed traffic, detection limits and maintenance deserve attention.

For the cleaning-robot side of the same question, see our guide to certifications for commercial cleaning robots, which compares floor-machine standards with ISO 3691-4.

What the risk assessment should cover

1. Operating zonesMap every area the vehicle enters — aisles, crossings, docks, lifts, mezzanine edges — and classify each by who else is present.
2. HazardsCollision with people and vehicles, crushing at stations, load falling, tipping on ramps, edges and drops, battery and charging hazards.
3. ControlsSpeed zones, safety-field settings, guarding, segregation, warning lights and sounds, emergency stops, keep-out zones.
4. Detection limitsWhat the vehicle does not reliably detect: low or overhanging objects, glass, reflective surfaces, floor defects.
5. PeopleTraining for operators and everyone who works near the robots; manual recovery procedures.
6. Change controlWhich changes to routes, stations, loads or software trigger a new assessment, and who signs it off.

A practical pilot plan

A pilot should test the real risks on site, not just prove the robot can drive. Pick one representative route with real floors, real loads, real shift traffic and at least one exception. Measure queue time, blocked-path recovery, docking success, manual interventions, battery behaviour, operator acceptance and tasks completed per shift.

For an AMR pilot, include map creation and a map change: move a pallet, block a lane, change a staging point and see whether the robot reroutes safely or causes congestion. For an AGV pilot, test guide-path durability, stop-zone discipline, intersection rules and the work needed to change the route. In both, record what happens when a person steps into the path, a load is badly placed, a station is occupied or Wi-Fi drops.

End with a go/no-go scorecard. “Demo successful” is not enough: record throughput, exception rate, safety observations, required site changes, integration gaps, training needs, spare parts and the cost of scaling from one route to a fleet.

FAQ

What is the difference between an AMR and an AGV?
An AGV follows a guide path built into the site — tape, wire, QR codes or reflectors — and stops when it is blocked. An AMR localises against a map with lidar and cameras, plans its own route and can re-plan around obstacles. Changing an AGV route means changing the floor; changing an AMR route means editing the map.
Is an AMR better than an AGV?
Not always. AMRs are stronger when layouts, traffic, missions and pickup points change. AGVs remain a good choice for stable, repetitive routes with controlled traffic and a long asset life.
Is an AGV cheaper than an AMR?
Not necessarily. AGV projects spend more on guide-path engineering, markers and protected lanes; AMR projects spend more on software, mapping and integration. Compare the cost per automated route over the system’s life, including the cost of changing routes.
Can AMRs and AGVs work on mezzanines or use lifts?
Yes, if the mezzanine is rated for the robot and load, open edges are guarded or protected by validated keep-out zones, and lift thresholds, gaps and door widths are within the robot’s limits. AMRs usually integrate more easily with lift and door control through their fleet software.
What does an AMR or AGV risk assessment cover?
Operating zones, hazards such as collision, crushing at stations, falling loads and edges, the controls for each hazard, the vehicle’s detection limits, training and manual recovery, and which changes trigger a new assessment. ISO 3691-4 and ANSI/A3 R15.08 are the usual references.
Do AMRs need warehouse management software integration?
A small pilot can run from call buttons or a simple fleet dashboard. Multi-shift production usually needs integration with WMS, MES or ERP, plus conveyors, lifts, doors and charging rules.
Can AGVs and AMRs from different vendors share one fleet manager?
Increasingly, yes, through VDA 5050, an open interface between vehicles and a master control system. Support varies by vendor and version, so confirm it in the quote; the AMR-0600 supports VDA 5050.
What payload should I choose?
Choose by the real load, rack or trolley footprint, centre of gravity, floor condition, threshold height, docking tolerance and future SKU mix — not by maximum kilograms alone.
Are AGVs safer than AMRs?
Neither is inherently safer. Safety depends on the system design, risk assessment, speed, sensing, traffic separation, maintenance, training and the operating environment. Both need documented safeguards and validation.
What should an AMR or AGV quote include?
Robot configuration, payload module, chargers, batteries, fleet software, mapping, integrations, safety documents, commissioning, training, spare parts, warranty, service response and shipping documents.
