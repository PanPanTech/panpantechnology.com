How the calculation works

The calculator values the manual hours the robot takes over, subtracts the time people still spend supervising it, and compares the result with the robot's yearly cost.
→Manual hours taken over = floor area × robot share ÷ manual cleaning rate × cleanings per year.
→Labour value = those hours × hourly wage × (1 + payroll taxes and benefits).
→Robots needed = robot area ÷ (robot practical rate × hours available per cleaning), rounded up.
→Supervision cost = minutes per cleaning × cleanings per year × loaded wage, per robot.
→Robot cost per year = price ÷ write-off years + consumables and service.
→Payback = robot price ÷ (labour value − supervision − running cost) per month.

The default wage is the BLS median for janitors and building cleaners, $17.27 an hour in May 2024. Replace it with your own fully loaded rate or your cleaning contractor's hourly charge, which is usually higher.

Choosing realistic inputs

Use practical cleaning rates, not brochure maxima, and measure the share of your floor a robot can actually reach. Over-optimistic inputs are the main reason robot business cases disappoint.

If you clean with a walk-behind scrubber today, your manual rate is high and the robot's value is mainly the operator hours it frees. If you clean with mops, the manual rate is low and the hours taken over per square metre are much larger.

What the calculator leaves out

Quality, consistency and reporting have value the calculator does not count, and downtime, training and site changes have costs it does not count either.
→Consistency. A robot cleans the same route every night and logs it; a short-staffed shift may not.
→Reassigned labour. Hours taken over are only saved if the people are redeployed or the contract changes.
→Downtime. A robot waiting for a part does not clean. Ask about local spares and response times.
→Edges and touch-ups. Corners and edges still need people; the robot share input should allow for this.
→Financing. Leasing and RaaS change the cash profile; see our price guide for the options.

FAQ

How do you calculate the ROI of a robot floor scrubber?
Value the manual cleaning hours the robot takes over at your loaded wage, subtract supervision time and the robot's yearly cost (price over its write-off period plus consumables and service), and divide the robot price by the monthly saving to get the payback period.
What is a typical payback period for a cleaning robot?
One to three years on large floors cleaned daily. Small floors cleaned a few times a week may not pay back on labour alone.
Which input matters most?
The share of floor the robot can clean and how often the floor is cleaned. Together they decide how many manual hours the robot takes over.
Should I use the robot's maximum m²/h figure?
No. Use the practical rate on a real route with turns and obstacles. Maximum figures assume straight runs and can be twice the practical rate.
Does a cleaning robot remove the need for cleaning staff?
No. Someone still starts routes, empties bins, checks edges and handles exceptions. Budget 15–30 minutes of supervision per run.
Is my data stored when I use the calculator?
No. The calculation runs in your browser and nothing is sent to PanPanTech.
