Hotel & Hospitality Cleaning Robots: What They Do, Where They Fit and How to Roll Them Out

Hotel cleaning robots clean public floors — corridors, lobbies, restaurants and ballrooms — at night or off-peak, while housekeeping stays focused on rooms. With 65% of surveyed US hotels still short-staffed and housekeeping the hardest role to fill, this guide covers which robot fits which area, noise and carpet limits, lifts, ROI and how to roll out without disturbing guests.

What a hotel cleaning robot actually does

A hotel cleaning robot cleans the public floors of a hotel — corridors, lobbies, restaurants, ballrooms and back-of-house — on a schedule, usually at night or off-peak. It does not make beds or clean bathrooms, and it is not sent into occupied rooms. Those stay with housekeeping.

That split is the key to every successful hotel deployment. Room attendants spend their shift on rooms; the long, repetitive floor routes in corridors and public areas are the part of the workload a robot can take over without changing the guest experience.

The pressure behind the interest is staffing. In an American Hotel & Lodging Association survey covering December 2024 to January 2025, 65% of surveyed hotels still reported staffing shortages, and housekeeping was the most affected role, cited by 38%. When a hotel cannot fill housekeeping shifts, the first thing that slips is the public-area floor round.

→Guest corridors — nightly vacuuming of carpet runners while guests sleep.
→Lobbies and lift lobbies — hard-floor scrubbing and dust-mopping off-peak.
→Restaurants and breakfast rooms — cleaning under tables after service.
→Ballrooms, meeting rooms and exhibition halls — fast floor resets between events.
→Back-of-house — service corridors, staff canteens and loading areas.
→Exterior — windows and curtain walls, with facade cleaning robots.

Which cleaning robots hotels are using

Hotels that run cleaning robots use them on public floors, split by area, not as one machine for the whole property. Corridor carpet gets a quiet compact robot at night. Lobby hard floors get a scrubber in a quiet hour. Restaurants get a low robot with a station after service. Back-of-house hard floors get a louder machine. Guest rooms stay with housekeeping. On this range that split is the ACR-0350, the ACR-0520, the ACR-0290 and the ACR-0370.

Corridor carpets

Guest-corridor carpet is a nightly vacuum route, run once the floor has gone quiet. The robot for it is the ACR-0350. Operating noise on the product page is below 60 dB, and the body passes aisles down to 60 cm. Vacuum mode keeps a carpet runner presentable from night to night. It does not replace periodic deep extraction, and pile height still has to be checked on your own carpet.

Lobby hard floors

A lobby, a lift lobby and the entrance hard floor are a scrubbing and dust-mopping job. The ACR-0520 is the 6-in-1 robot built for offices, hotels and other public floors, with scrubbing capacity up to 1,197 m²/h. Published noise is 55 dB when scrubbing and 45 dB when dust-mopping, so an early-morning dust-mop is a different job from a scrub at midnight. Keep it off peak check-in and check-out.

Restaurants

Restaurant and breakfast floors are cleaned after service, under tables and along banquettes. The ACR-0290 is specified for restaurants and hotel public areas: 22.3 kg and 429 × 480 × 283 mm, so the 283 mm height gets it under a table. The station refills clean water, drains dirty water, and washes and dries the robot. The product page does not publish an operating-noise figure, so run it when the dining room is closed.

Back of house

Service corridors, staff canteens and loading-bay floors can be wetter and louder than anything a guest walks. The ACR-0370 is a 4-in-1 robot listed for hotels, with operating noise under 70 dB. That is reasonable behind a service door and the wrong level past guest-room doors at night.

Can a cleaning robot clean a guest room?

Not in the sense a guest means by “clean my room.” A hotel cleaning robot does not make the bed, replace amenities, clean the bathroom or work around someone in the room. Housekeeping still owns the room. What a robot can do is the open floor, and only when that floor has been made safe for it.

The ACR-0350 is the only model to consider for that limited job. The product page lists hotel guest areas and corridors, rates noise below 60 dB and passes a 60 cm aisle, so on a vacant room it can take the open floor. The room has to be empty. Bags, cables, loose rugs and the housekeeping cart are no-go items. It will not clean under a made bed in any useful way, and a bathroom stays with the attendant.

Most hotels should not plan in-room cleaning. The hours a robot reliably takes are the corridor runner, the first round dropped when a shift is short. If you test vacant rooms, do it on one departure block with a supervisor, and stop if clearing the floor takes as long as vacuuming it. A lobby, asked about in the same breath, is a robot job: hard floor, every day, and quiet enough early for the ACR-0520 at 45 dB when dust-mopping. A guest room is not a small lobby. It is occupied, and it is judged on the bed and the bathroom.

Noise, guests and timing

Noise decides when a robot can run. Under 60 dB is the practical target for corridors at night; lobbies and restaurants can use louder modes when they are empty.

Low noise: the decibel figures on the product pages

Low noise is a published number, and only some of these robots have one. The ACR-0350, for corridor carpet, is below 60 dB. That is the night-route figure past guest-room doors. The ACR-0520, for lobby hard floors, is 55 dB when scrubbing and 45 dB when dust-mopping. Dust-mop when the lobby is occupied; scrub in a quieter window. The ACR-0520V, for a hotel spa, gym or clinic, is below 55 dB. Its 222 nm far-UVC module switches on only along the travel path, after cleaning rather than instead of it. The ACR-0370, for back of house, is under 70 dB: fine behind the service door, not in a guest corridor. The ACR-0290 does not publish a noise figure, so it runs after service with the dining room closed. A few decibels over the night-corridor target is a machine the night manager will switch off. Test one floor, because corridor acoustics and door seals change what a guest hears.

Hotels are never empty, so the schedule is designed around occupancy rather than around the robot. A common pattern is corridors after 23:00 at the quietest setting, restaurants straight after breakfast and dinner service, lobbies in the early morning, and ballrooms whenever an event finishes.

→Check door seals and corridor acoustics. The same robot can sound different in two hotels. Test on one floor before scheduling a whole building.
→Use quiet modes in occupied areas. Many robots offer quieter modes; check how much suction or speed they give up.
→Avoid peak check-in and check-out. Lobbies are busiest mid-morning and mid-afternoon.
→Tell guests what the robot is. A small sign or a line in the welcome information prevents complaints and photos on social media from being misread.

Carpet, lifts and other hotel practicalities

Most hotel robots vacuum carpet well but do not replace periodic deep extraction. Lifts are the biggest practical limit: unless the robot is integrated with the lift, staff move it between floors.
→Carpet. Robots with vacuum modes keep corridor carpet clean day to day. Deep shampoo or extraction remains a periodic manual job. Send a photo of your carpet so the pile can be checked.
→Lifts. Cleaning robots are usually assigned per floor or moved by staff. Multi-floor autonomy needs lift integration, which is common on delivery robots and AMRs but should be confirmed per lift model.
→Thresholds and rugs. Door thresholds, loose rugs and cables are the usual cause of stuck robots. Mark them as no-go zones during mapping.
→Water and drain. A station that connects to water and drain, like the one on the ACR-0520P, removes the daily refill job entirely.
→Storage and charging. Plan a charging spot per floor or per zone, near a socket and out of guest sight lines.

What a hotel robot is worth

A hotel robot pays back when it reliably takes over hours of public-area floor work every night. Value it in housekeeping hours released to rooms, not in headcount reduction.

US BLS data for maids and housekeeping cleaners gives the wage benchmark for this work. A robot that takes over three hours of corridor vacuuming a night releases more than 1,000 hours a year — time that can go back into room turnover, where staff shortages hurt guest scores most.

→Hours taken over per night — the biggest input. Measure the current corridor and public-area round.
→Supervision time — someone still starts routes, empties bins and checks edges. Budget 15–30 minutes a night.
→Robot cost per year — purchase price over five years plus consumables and service, or a RaaS fee.
→Quality — consistent nightly coverage that is logged, instead of a round that is skipped when a shift is short.

How to roll out robots in an operating hotel

Start with one floor or one venue, measure for two to four weeks, then extend. The pilot answers the questions no spec sheet can: noise in your corridors, carpet results and staff acceptance.
→Walk-through — floor types, carpet pile, corridor widths, lifts, thresholds and storage spots.
→Quiet-hours plan — which areas robots clean at night, which off-peak, which stay manual.
→Pilot — one floor or venue for two to four weeks; track housekeeping hours and guest comments.
→Handover — housekeeping supervisors learn scheduling and station care; fault alerts go to the duty manager.
→Extend — add floors and properties; compare them on the same reports in the fleet cloud.

FAQ

What is a hotel cleaning robot?
An autonomous robot that cleans a hotel's public floors — corridors, lobbies, restaurants, ballrooms and back-of-house — on a schedule. It does not make beds or clean bathrooms, and occupied guest rooms remain with housekeeping. A vacant room's open floor is a limited case, not the main job.
Are cleaning robots quiet enough for hotel corridors at night?
Robots rated below 60 dB, such as the ACR-0350, are suitable for night corridor routes. Test on one floor first, because corridor acoustics and door seals vary.
Can a hotel cleaning robot clean carpet?
Robots with vacuum modes keep corridor carpet clean day to day. Periodic deep extraction is still a manual job.
Can a cleaning robot use the hotel lifts?
Usually not by default. Cleaning robots are assigned per floor or moved by staff unless lift integration is installed and confirmed for your lift model.
Do cleaning robots work in resorts with several buildings?
Yes, with one robot — or one fleet group — assigned per building or floor, because most cleaning robots do not cross outdoor paths or use lifts unless integration is installed. Conference centres and ballrooms suit larger scrubbers; guest-wing corridors suit compact vacuum-and-mop robots.
Will robots replace housekeeping staff?
No. They take over repetitive public-area floor routes so housekeeping can concentrate on rooms and guests. See our article on whether cleaning robots will replace human cleaners.
How much does a hotel cleaning robot cost?
Compact and mid-size commercial cleaning robots typically list between about $15,000 and $45,000 in 2026, with RaaS options from about $600 a month. Large scrubbers for ballrooms cost more. These are market list prices from public reseller guides, not a PanPanTech quote; our commercial cleaning robot price guide shows how they break down.
Which cleaning robots are hotels using?
Hotels use different robots for different floors. Corridor carpet is a quiet compact robot such as the ACR-0350 (below 60 dB, 60 cm aisles). Lobby hard floors suit the ACR-0520 (55 dB when scrubbing, 45 dB when dust-mopping, up to 1,197 m²/h). Restaurants after service suit the ACR-0290 (22.3 kg, 283 mm tall, self-washing station). Back-of-house hard floors suit the ACR-0370, rated under 70 dB. Guest rooms stay with housekeeping.
Are hotel cleaning robots low noise?
The quiet models publish these figures: ACR-0350 below 60 dB, ACR-0520 at 55 dB when scrubbing and 45 dB when dust-mopping, and ACR-0520V below 55 dB. The ACR-0370 is under 70 dB and belongs in back of house, not in a guest corridor at night. The ACR-0290 does not publish an operating-noise figure. Test one floor, because corridor acoustics change what guests hear.
Can a cleaning robot clean guest rooms and lobbies?
Lobbies, yes. They are hard floors cleaned off-peak, and the ACR-0520 is the model for that, including 45 dB when dust-mopping. Guest rooms, only in a narrow sense. A robot does not make the bed, clean the bathroom or work in an occupied room. On a vacant room with the floor cleared, the ACR-0350 can vacuum or mop the open floor. Most hotels keep the robot in the corridor.
Which hotel cleaning robot model is a good fit?
Match the model to the floor. For PanPanTech that is the ACR-0350 on guest-corridor carpet, the ACR-0520 on lobby hard floors, the ACR-0290 in restaurants and the ACR-0370 in back of house. A spa, gym or clinic can add the ACR-0520V (below 55 dB, 222 nm far-UVC on the travel path only). Large ballrooms use the ACR-0800, rated up to 4,000 m²/h.
Which hotel areas should we automate first?
Guest corridors at night and restaurant floors after service. Both are repetitive, happen every day and are easy to measure before and after.
